Medallion Architecture

Medallion architecture organizes data through a series of progressive refinement layers — each named after increasingly precious metals. Raw data enters at Bronze, is cleaned and standardized at Silver, aggregated into business metrics at Gold, and delivered as a polished product at Platinum. Each layer adds structure and meaning, making the data more reliable and easier to consume at every step.

This guide walks through each layer as built in the Hahn Family Farm pipeline — ingesting NOAA daily weather observations for Gage County, Nebraska and transforming them into growing-season agricultural insights. Click any layer button above for a detailed architecture diagram of that stage.

Bronze — Raw Ingestion
Job: NoaaIncrementalUpdateJob  ·  Output: data/bronze/noaa_observations/

What it means

Bronze is the landing zone for raw data. Nothing is changed — units, formats, and field names stay exactly as the source delivers them. The goal is completeness and reproducibility: if something goes wrong downstream, you can always re-derive every result from Bronze without calling the API again.

In this project

The NOAA NCEI API returns daily observations in long format — one row per station, date, and data type. Bronze stores these rows as-is in a Delta Lake table. Temperatures are in °C, precipitation in mm.

The anchor station is Beatrice 1 N, NE (USC00250622). The incremental update job also fetches all Gage County stations plus the Lincoln Airport ASOS (USW00014939) as a supplemental temperature source.

Silver — Cleaning & Conforming
Job: WeatherTransformJob  ·  Output: data/silver/weather_observations/

What it means

Silver cleans and standardizes Bronze data. Columns are given consistent types, units are converted to a common standard, and gaps are filled using defined fallback rules. The result is a wide-format table that downstream consumers can trust without worrying about which station the data came from or what units it uses.

In this project

Bronze's long-format rows (one per station/date/type) are pivoted to wide format (one row per date). Temperatures convert °C → °F; precipitation converts mm → inches.

A three-tier temperature fallback fills gaps in order of geographic priority: (1) anchor station → (2) Gage County pool median → (3) Lincoln ASOS supplemental. A tempSource column records which tier was used on each date.

Gold — Business Aggregation
Job: SeasonWeatherJob  ·  Output: data/gold/season_weather/

What it means

Gold applies business logic to produce the metrics that stakeholders care about. Daily records are aggregated to the right grain — here, annual growing-season summaries. Gold answers the core question: how did this growing season compare to past years?

In this project

Silver daily records are rolled up by year into growing-season statistics covering April–October. Growing Degree Days (GDD) measure accumulated heat useful for corn development. Heat stress days count days above 95°F — a known yield risk.

The pipeline covers 2000–2012, the years when the anchor station reported full temperature data. It became precipitation-only in 2013.

Key metric — Growing Degree Days (GDD)

GDD is the primary indicator of crop development. High-temperature days are capped (corn stops benefiting above 86°F) and cold nights are floored (no development below 50°F).

Platinum — Presentation
Job: PlatinumExportJob  ·  Output: platinum/index.html

What it means

Platinum is the consumption layer — polished, ready-to-use output delivered in the form end users need. It never stores raw or intermediate data; it transforms Gold metrics into a finished product optimized for its audience. Anyone who needs the answer does not need to know how the pipeline works.

In this project

Gold annual summaries are rendered into a self-contained Plotly.js HTML dashboard — one file, no server, no dependencies. Charts include year-over-year GDD, precipitation, and temperature trends with interactive hover, zoom, and crop selection.

The entire dashboard is a single index.html that anyone on the family can open in a browser and explore.

What gets embedded in the dashboard

Growing Degree Days

Year-over-year GDD totals showing warm vs. cool growing seasons. 2012 stands out as the hottest and driest year in the record.

Precipitation

Annual totals and monthly breakdowns — drought years (2012) and wet years (2010) are immediately visible in the trend chart.

Heat Stress Days

Count of days above 95°F during the growing season. A direct risk factor for corn pollination and yield — mapped per year.

Monthly Temperatures

Daily and monthly average temperatures for May through September — the critical months for corn and soy development.

Daily Detail

Interactive daily chart with precipitation bars and temperature lines. Gaps show where station data is missing for that date.

Crop Toggle

Sidebar controls let you filter charts by crop (corn, soy, winter wheat) and select individual years for comparison.
